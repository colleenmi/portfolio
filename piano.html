ASME Hybrid Piano Project

Club Involvement - American Society of Mechanical Engineers UIUC

November 2025 - April 2026

For UIUC Engineering Open House 2026 theme Forging the Future, I co-led a digital hybrid piano project with my friend Noah as part of the American Society of Mechanical Engineers.

Each year the University of Illinois at Urbana Champaign hosts Engineering Open House, a two‑day fair where students present personal and research projects to the public. Our team designed a silent system piano that functions acoustically but can also mute its sound and produce digital audio through sensors and microcontrollers.

Commercial silent systems cost around $2,000. Our goal was to build a fully DIY hybrid piano for roughly $300 while preserving normal acoustic operation and adding a digital output for headphones and recording.

Team and roles

We worked with a recurring team of 15–20 members organized into mechanical, electrical, and sound subteams. I coordinated the overall project and led the integration between subteams.

Mechanical design

The mechanical subteam designed a removable mute mechanism that fully silences the piano when engaged. Using Fusion 360, we modeled a compact rack and pinion assembly driven by two DC 48:1 gearbox motors. The motors drive pinion gears along 3D printed racks to push a large dowel that moves the hammers away from the strings, effectively muting the acoustic sound while remaining removable for maintenance.

We also redesigned grommets for the lifter wires on the spinet piano and 3D printed them in TPU to match rubber-like elasticity while allowing fine adjustment.

Electrical and sensing

To avoid the cost of 176 commercial sensors, the electrical team applied copper tape to the backs of the keys to create simple on/off contacts. An Arduino Uno measured the time between contact connect and disconnect to detect note activation, release, and striking velocity. By mapping the measured milliseconds to volume, the system produced dynamic contrast: longer times correspond to softer dynamics and shorter times to louder dynamics.

We built 11 proto boards with shift registers, each handling eight keys, and routed binary data to an audio module that converted signals to MIDI. The proto boards included capacitors and debouncing measures to reduce false triggers.

Sound integration and prototype

The sound team mapped Arduino outputs to piano notes and tested a demo board that produced digital audio from the sensor signals. Midway through the timeline we had a functioning prototype that demonstrated both digital and acoustic operation.

What we learned

Students gained hands-on experience in iterative 3D printing, embedded electronics, wire management, soldering, PCB prototyping, troubleshooting daisy chained boards, and communicating technical concepts to a general audience. This was my first time leading a technical project; the role involved many iterations, late-night decisions, and a new appreciation for PCB design and careful mechanical tolerancing.

Although the system was not fully complete by the final demo, we achieved a functional mute rail and roughly 30 working keys. The project remains the most challenging I have led and a major learning experience.
